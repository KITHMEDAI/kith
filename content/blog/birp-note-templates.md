A BIRP note template organizes a session around four sections: Behavior, Intervention, Response, Plan. It's a close cousin of SOAP and DAP, but built around a slightly different question — not just what the patient reported and what you observed, but what you *did* in the session and how the patient responded to it. That makes BIRP a common default in settings that lean heavily on documented interventions, like community mental health centers and substance use treatment programs, where a note often needs to justify the clinical work performed, not just summarize the conversation.

None of the major note formats is more clinically correct than the others — it comes down to what your setting, supervisor, or funding source expects. Here's a BIRP template you can use directly, what belongs in each section, and where people tend to trip up.

## The BIRP note format: four sections, briefly

**Behavior (B)** — what the patient presented with and reported this session: their stated mood, symptoms, and any significant events, combined with what you directly observed (affect, presentation, engagement). This plays a similar role to SOAP's Subjective and Objective, or DAP's Data — the factual, descriptive layer before your clinical read.

**Intervention (I)** — what you actually did in the session. The specific technique, modality, or approach you used: a cognitive reframe, a grounding exercise, psychoeducation on a topic, a motivational interviewing prompt. This is BIRP's distinguishing section — SOAP and DAP don't have a dedicated place for documenting the clinician's own action, and BIRP makes it explicit.

**Response (R)** — how the patient responded to that intervention specifically. Did they engage with it, resist it, have an insight, stay flat? This is different from Behavior at the top of the note — Behavior covers the session broadly, Response is tied directly to what you tried.

**Plan (P)** — what happens next. Homework or between-session tasks, focus for the next session, any changes to the treatment approach, referrals if applicable.

## A blank template

```
BIRP Note — [Date] — Session #[N]

BEHAVIOR
- Presenting concerns this session:
- Reported mood/symptoms (patient's own words):
- Significant events since last session:
- Affect/presentation observed:
- Engagement level:

INTERVENTION
- Technique/approach used this session:
- Rationale (why this intervention, briefly):

RESPONSE
- Patient's response to the intervention:
- Insight, resistance, or shift observed:

PLAN
- Homework/between-session tasks:
- Focus for next session:
- Changes to treatment approach:
- Referrals (if any):
```

## A worked example

A fully fictional, generic vignette, not a real case:

```
BIRP Note — 12 October 2026 — Session #7

BEHAVIOR
- Presenting concerns: increased irritability at work, disrupted sleep
- Reported mood: "on edge most of the week"
- Significant events: missed a deadline for the first time, describes
  feeling "embarrassed" about it
- Affect: tense at the start of session, loosened somewhat by midpoint
- Engagement: good; brought up the missed deadline unprompted

INTERVENTION
- Used cognitive reframing to examine the "I'm failing at everything"
  thought that followed the missed deadline
- Introduced a brief breathing exercise to use before high-stress
  work moments

RESPONSE
- Engaged well with the reframing exercise; identified on their own
  that one missed deadline doesn't match "failing at everything"
- Tried the breathing exercise in session; reported it felt
  "a little awkward but okay"

PLAN
- Practice breathing exercise daily before checking email
- Track irritability triggers for next session
- No changes to treatment approach at this time
```

Notice that Intervention names the specific technique, not just "discussed coping strategies" — and Response ties directly back to that technique rather than restating the patient's overall mood. That link between what you did and how it landed is what makes a BIRP note different from a SOAP or DAP note covering the same session.

## BIRP vs. SOAP vs. DAP: which one should you use

The practical difference is where each format puts its emphasis. SOAP separates reported and observed information into two sections and keeps interpretation in Assessment. DAP merges reported and observed into one Data section for speed. BIRP is built around the intervention itself — it asks you to name what you did and document the patient's response to that specific action, which SOAP and DAP don't require explicitly.

That makes BIRP a natural fit when your documentation needs to demonstrate active treatment — for insurance review, agency audit, or supervision — rather than just narrate the session. If your setting doesn't require that emphasis, SOAP or DAP's simpler description-then-interpretation structure can be faster to write.

If you're not currently required to use one or the other, it's worth picking based on which one you find yourself writing more completely under time pressure, since a note you finish beats a more detailed format you abandon halfway through.

## Why the format you use actually matters

It's easy to treat the choice of note format as a formality — pick one, move on. But the format shapes what you actually capture, because a template is a checklist as much as it's a layout. BIRP's Intervention section, in particular, only works if you're specific: "used CBT" tells a future reader almost nothing, while "used cognitive reframing on the 'I'm failing at everything' thought" tells them exactly what happened and gives them something to build on next session.

The other reason format consistency matters: whoever reads the note later — a supervisor doing a chart review, an auditor, a colleague covering for you unexpectedly — is relying on the structure to find what they need quickly. A BIRP note that skips Intervention some weeks and not others, or blurs Response into Behavior, is harder to trust than one that's boringly consistent every time.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

**Vague interventions.** "Provided support" or "used therapeutic techniques" isn't specific enough to be useful on review. Name the actual technique.

**Response that just repeats Behavior.** If Response reads like a copy of the mood description from the top of the note, it's not doing its job — it should describe how the patient reacted to the specific intervention you named.

**Treating Behavior as a transcript.** Behavior should be a focused summary of what's clinically relevant, not a blow-by-blow of the session. If it runs long enough that Intervention and Response feel like an afterthought, it probably needs trimming.

**Vague Plans.** "Continue treatment as before" isn't a plan — it's the absence of one. A useful Plan section names something concrete that's different about next time, even if it's small.

A consistent BIRP structure, like SOAP or DAP, isn't really about satisfying a documentation requirement — it's about making sure a patient's chart, read end to end, actually shows what was tried and how it worked, rather than a string of similar-sounding entries.
