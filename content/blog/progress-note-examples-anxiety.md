Progress note examples for anxiety are useful for a specific reason: anxiety sessions are easy to document badly. The presentation shifts week to week, a lot of the work happens in the patient's head rather than in visible behavior, and it's tempting to fall back on phrases like "patient reports anxiety, discussed coping skills" that say almost nothing to a future reader. Below is one fictional session written three ways — SOAP, DAP, and BIRP — so you can see how the same clinical material lands in each format, followed by what's worth capturing in anxiety-focused notes specifically.

All three examples describe the same invented, generic vignette. They're not drawn from a real case, and they're not a substitute for your own clinical judgment or your setting's documentation requirements.

## The session behind the examples

A patient in their early thirties, seen weekly for generalized worry, is in their sixth session. This week they describe worry about work performance that's been spilling into evenings, trouble falling asleep most nights, and one episode of a racing heart and tight chest before a team presentation. In session, you revisit the worry log they started last week, work through one of the recorded worries using a cognitive restructuring approach, and practise a paced-breathing exercise. They leave with a plan to keep the worry log and try scheduled "worry time" in the evenings.

## Anxiety progress note example: SOAP

```
SOAP Note — 20 October 2026 — Session #6

SUBJECTIVE
- Reports worry about work performance "most of the day," continuing
  into evenings
- Difficulty falling asleep 5 of the last 7 nights
- One episode before a team presentation: racing heart, tight chest,
  urge to leave the room; stayed and presented
- Completed worry log on 4 of 7 days
- Denies thoughts of self-harm

OBJECTIVE
- Alert, oriented, appropriately dressed
- Fidgeting and rapid speech in the first part of the session;
  visibly calmer after breathing exercise
- Engaged, brought worry log to session

ASSESSMENT
- Generalized worry remains the primary concern, now affecting sleep
- Presentation episode suggests anticipatory anxiety around
  performance situations; notable that patient stayed rather than
  avoided
- Good engagement with between-session tasks

PLAN
- Continue worry log daily
- Introduce scheduled 20-minute evening "worry time"
- Practise paced breathing before sleep
- Next session: review sleep pattern, continue cognitive restructuring
  on performance-related worries
```

SOAP keeps what the patient told you (Subjective) separate from what you saw (Objective). For anxiety, that split is worth having: the patient saying "I'm fine" while fidgeting and speaking rapidly is clinically meaningful, and SOAP gives you a natural place to record both. ## Anxiety progress note example: DAP

```
DAP Note — 20 October 2026 — Session #6

DATA
- Reports ongoing work-related worry, spilling into evenings; sleep
  onset difficulty 5 of 7 nights
- One episode before a presentation (racing heart, tight chest, urge
  to leave); stayed and presented
- Completed worry log 4 of 7 days
- Fidgety with rapid speech early in session, calmer after paced
  breathing; engaged throughout
- Denies thoughts of self-harm
- Session: reviewed worry log, cognitive restructuring on "if I make
  one mistake they'll see I'm not good enough," practised paced
  breathing

ASSESSMENT
- Worry pattern stable in content but now disrupting sleep
- Anticipatory anxiety in performance situations; did not avoid,
  which is a meaningful strength to build on
- Responded well to restructuring — generated a balanced alternative
  thought with modest prompting

PLAN
- Continue worry log daily
- Trial 20-minute scheduled worry time each evening
- Paced breathing before bed
- Next session: review sleep, continue restructuring on performance
  worries
```

DAP folds reported and observed information into one Data section, which makes it faster to write — the trade-off is that it's easier to lose the distinction between what the patient said and what you saw. The [DAP note template](/blog/dap-note-templates) post covers the format in more detail.

## Anxiety progress note example: BIRP

```
BIRP Note — 20 October 2026 — Session #6

BEHAVIOR
- Reports work-related worry most of the day, continuing into
  evenings; sleep onset difficulty 5 of 7 nights
- One episode before a presentation (racing heart, tight chest, urge
  to leave); stayed and presented
- Fidgety, rapid speech early in session
- Denies thoughts of self-harm

INTERVENTION
- Reviewed worry log (completed 4 of 7 days)
- Cognitive restructuring on the thought "if I make one mistake
  they'll see I'm not good enough"
- Guided paced-breathing exercise (in-session practice)

RESPONSE
- Identified two pieces of evidence against the thought; generated
  a balanced alternative with modest prompting
- Noticeably calmer after breathing exercise; said it was "easier
  than expected"

PLAN
- Continue worry log daily
- Trial 20-minute scheduled worry time each evening
- Paced breathing before bed
- Next session: review sleep, continue restructuring
```

BIRP's distinguishing feature is the explicit link between what you did and how the patient responded to it. For anxiety work, where much of the session is structured technique — restructuring, exposure planning, breathing, grounding — that link is often the most useful thing in the chart. ## What to document in anxiety progress notes

Whichever format you use, a few things tend to make anxiety notes more useful on review.

**Specific symptoms, not just the label.** "Anxious" tells a future reader very little. Sleep disruption, physical symptoms, avoidance, reassurance-seeking, and how often these occur give a much clearer picture — and make it possible to see change over time.

**Frequency and function, where you can.** "Sleep onset difficulty 5 of 7 nights" is easier to compare next month than "trouble sleeping." You don't need precision for everything, but a rough count on the one or two symptoms you're targeting gives the chart a baseline.

**Avoidance and approach.** Anxiety notes often record what the patient felt but not what they *did*. Whether someone stayed in the presentation or left is clinically significant, and it's one of the clearest markers of progress.

**Standardised measures, if you use them.** If your practice uses a screening or outcome measure such as the GAD-7, note the score and date in the note rather than only in a separate file, so the trend is visible in the session record itself.

**Risk, briefly and every time it's relevant.** A short line stating what you asked and what the patient said is standard practice in most settings. Follow your own service's risk documentation policy — a progress note example isn't a risk assessment framework.

**The specific intervention.** "Discussed coping strategies" could describe almost any session. Naming the thought you worked on or the technique you practised makes the next session easier to plan.

## Common mistakes in anxiety progress notes

**Copying last week's note forward.** Anxiety presentations can look similar session to session, which makes copy-paste tempting. It also makes the chart useless for tracking change — and it's the kind of thing an auditor or supervisor notices quickly.

**Only recording distress.** Notes that list symptoms but never strengths or approach behaviours give a lopsided picture. The patient who stayed in the room despite a racing heart did something worth writing down.

**Over-quoting.** A short quote in the patient's own words can be valuable. A paragraph of transcript isn't — it buries the clinical content and adds sensitive detail the note doesn't need.

**Vague plans.** "Continue CBT" isn't a plan. "Trial 20-minute scheduled worry time in the evenings" is.
