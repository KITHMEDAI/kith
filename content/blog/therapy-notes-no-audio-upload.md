A lot of therapists like the idea of AI help with session notes but stop short at one step: recording the session, saving the file, and uploading it to someone's server afterwards. If that's where you hesitate, you're not being overly cautious. An audio file of a therapy session is about as sensitive as clinical data gets. The good news is that plenty of workflows don't involve uploading a recording at all. Before you pick a therapy notes tool with no audio upload, though, it helps to know exactly what "no upload" means in each case, because the phrase covers quite different things.

## Three ways AI note tools get session content

Most AI documentation tools fall into one of three groups, depending on how the session reaches the AI:

1. **Upload-based tools.** You record the session yourself, with a phone, a recorder or your video platform's recording feature, and then upload the audio file. The tool transcribes it and writes a note. This is the workflow most people picture, and it's the one this post is about avoiding.
2. **Live (streaming) transcription tools.** The tool listens while the session happens, through your device microphone in the room or a notetaker bot on a video call. Audio is sent for transcription as it's spoken. You never save or upload a file, but audio does still leave your device in real time.
3. **Input-based tools.** Nothing is recorded or streamed. You type or paste your own rough notes after the session, and the AI turns them into a structured note. We've covered this approach in detail in [AI therapy notes without recording](/blog/ai-therapy-notes-without-recording).

Groups 2 and 3 are both "no audio upload", but they're very different from a privacy and consent point of view. Lumping them together is where most of the confusion starts.

## Why therapists avoid the audio-upload workflow

The reasons usually come down to a few practical concerns:

- **A file exists that didn't need to.** Once you've recorded a session, there's an audio file on your phone or laptop, possibly also synced to a personal cloud backup, as well as on the vendor's server. Each copy is something you're responsible for.
- **You have to manage deletion.** With upload-based tools you need to know how long the vendor keeps the file, whether deleting it in the app really deletes it, and whether your own local copy has been cleared.
- **It adds steps.** Recording, saving, finding the file, uploading and waiting for processing is friction at the end of a long day, and it's the kind of friction that makes notes pile up.
- **Personal devices weren't designed for this.** A session recorded on the phone you also use for messaging and photos is hard to keep separate from everything else on it.

None of this makes upload-based tools wrong. Some handle retention carefully. But the workflow does create more copies of sensitive data, and more places for it to end up.

## Option 1: live transcription with no stored recording

Live transcription removes the "file" from the picture. The audio is processed as it's spoken and turned into text, and a well-designed tool doesn't keep the audio once the transcript exists. You still get the main benefits of capture: details you'd otherwise forget, the client's own words for quotes, and fuller notes from back-to-back days. We explain how this works in our guide to [ambient session transcription](/blog/ambient-session-transcription).

What to check:

- **Is the audio kept after transcription?** "Real time" doesn't automatically mean "not stored". Ask the vendor directly, and look for the answer in their privacy policy, not just the marketing page.
- **What about online sessions?** Notetaker bots that join video calls are often run by a third-party provider. Find out whether that provider holds recording data, even briefly, and for how long.
- **Is the transcript stored?** Usually yes, because the note is built from it. That's a smaller and more editable record than audio, but it's still sensitive clinical content.
- **Consent still applies.** Streaming audio is still recording in the sense that matters to your client. You need their informed consent just as you would for a saved file.

## Option 2: typed input, so no audio at all

If you'd rather no audio leaves the room in any form, an input-based tool is the cleanest choice. You write what you'd have written anyway, even messy shorthand, and the AI handles the structure. This suits clients who decline recording, sessions in settings where capture isn't practical, and clinicians who find that writing a few lines after a session is part of how they reflect.

The trade-off is that the note can only contain what you wrote down. There are no verbatim quotes unless you noted them, and nothing the client mentioned in passing that you didn't capture. A good tool should leave thin sections thin rather than invent detail. It's worth testing that with deliberately sparse input before you rely on one.

## Questions to ask any no-upload therapy notes tool

Whichever approach you're looking at, these questions sort out the careful tools from the rest:

- **What exactly leaves my device?** Audio, a transcript, your typed notes, or only the finished note?
- **What is stored, where, and for how long?** Ask about audio, transcripts and generated notes separately. They often have different retention rules.
- **Are separate consents built in?** Recording and AI processing are two different things a client can agree to. A tool that tracks both separately makes your consent conversation easier to evidence.
- **Who else touches the data?** Transcription providers, AI model providers and meeting-bot providers are all sub-processors. The vendor should be able to name them.
- **Does it fit your jurisdiction?** Indian therapists should be thinking about the DPDP Act, and UK therapists about UK GDPR. See our posts on [DPDP 2023 and therapist notes](/blog/dpdp-2023-therapist-notes) and [GDPR and AI notetaking in the UK](/blog/gdpr-ai-notetaking-uk).
- **Can you export your notes?** If you leave the tool, your records should come with you in a usable format.

## Choosing the right workflow

A simple way to decide:

- **Choose live transcription without stored audio** if you want fuller notes and direct quotes, your clients are comfortable consenting, and you'd like to avoid managing recording files.
- **Choose typed input** if no audio should leave the room, for a particular client or as a rule, and you already jot brief notes after sessions.
- **Use both** if your caseload varies. Many therapists capture sessions for clients who agree and write rough notes for those who don't.

Whichever you choose, the real goal of avoiding audio upload is to keep fewer copies of sensitive data in fewer places. Ask vendors specific questions, get the answers in writing, and review every note before it goes into the record.
