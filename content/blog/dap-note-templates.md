A DAP note template is the three-section cousin of SOAP: Data, Assessment, Plan. Instead of splitting what the patient reported (Subjective) from what you observed (Objective) into two sections, DAP merges both into a single "Data" section, then keeps Assessment and Plan largely the same. Some practices and training programs prefer it because it's faster to write and reads more like a single narrative of the session rather than four separate boxes to fill in.

Neither format is more clinically rigorous than the other — it usually comes down to what your supervisor, employer, or insurance panel expects. Here's a DAP note template you can use directly, what belongs in each section, and where people tend to trip up.

## The DAP note format: three sections, briefly

**Data (D)** — everything factual about the session: what the patient reported (their words, mood, symptoms, significant events) combined with what you directly observed (affect, presentation, engagement, any standardized measure scores). This is the merged version of SOAP's Subjective and Objective — reported and observed facts, without your interpretation of them yet.

**Assessment (A)** — your clinical read on the Data. Progress toward treatment goals, changes since the last session, clinical impressions, risk considerations if relevant. This is the section where you're allowed to interpret; everything above it should stay factual.

**Plan (P)** — what happens next. Homework or between-session tasks, focus for the next session, any changes to the treatment approach, referrals if applicable.

## A worked example

A fully fictional, generic vignette, not a real case:

```
DAP Note — 6 October 2026 — Session #4

DATA
- Presenting concerns: conflict with a family member, low motivation
- Reported mood: "drained, but not as bad as two weeks ago"
- Significant events: had the conversation with their sibling they'd
  been avoiding; describes it as "awkward but not a disaster"
- Affect: brighter than previous session, some spontaneous humor
- Engagement: moderate; needed some prompting to expand on the
  sibling conversation
- Standardized measures: not administered this session

ASSESSMENT
- Willingness to initiate the avoided conversation suggests some
  reduction in avoidance behavior tied to treatment goal #2
- Motivation remains low outside of relational stressors; possible
  target for next phase of treatment
- No risk indicators present this session

PLAN
- Explore motivation/low-energy pattern further next session
- Follow up on how the sibling relationship develops post-conversation
- No changes to treatment approach at this time
```

Notice that the Data section holds both what the patient said and what was observed, without editorializing — "brighter than previous session" is an observation, not a conclusion. The conclusions belong in Assessment. That boundary is what keeps a DAP note useful instead of repetitive.

## DAP vs. SOAP: which one should you use

The practical difference is just where the line falls: SOAP separates reported and observed information into two sections; DAP merges them into one. If your patient's report and your observations tend to align closely, DAP's single Data section can feel more natural and a little faster to write. If they often diverge — the patient reports feeling fine but presents as visibly distressed, for instance — SOAP's separation can make that gap easier to spot on a re-read.

If you're not currently required to use one or the other, it's worth picking based on which one you find yourself writing more completely under time pressure, since a note you finish beats a more elegant format you abandon halfway through.

## Why the format you use actually matters

It's easy to treat the choice between DAP and SOAP as a formality — pick one, move on. But the format shapes what you actually capture, because a template is a checklist as much as it's a layout. A single merged Data section makes it faster to write "patient reported X, appeared Y" in one breath, but it also makes it easier to blur the two together over time, especially on a day when you're writing notes back-to-back between sessions. If you notice your Data sections increasingly reading like pure interpretation rather than fact, that's worth catching regardless of which format your practice has settled on.

The other reason format consistency matters: whoever reads the note later — a supervisor doing a chart review, an insurer requesting records, a colleague covering for you unexpectedly — is relying on the structure to find what they need quickly. A DAP note that wanders into SOAP-style subsections partway through, or that skips Assessment some weeks and not others, is harder to trust than one that's boringly consistent every time.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

**Letting Assessment leak into Data.** If you write "patient seems to be minimizing" in the Data section, that's an interpretation, not a fact — move it to Assessment.

**Treating Data as a transcript.** Data should be a focused summary of what's clinically relevant, not a blow-by-blow of the session. If a section runs long enough that the Assessment underneath it feels like an afterthought, the Data section probably needs trimming.

**Vague Plans.** "Continue treatment as before" isn't a plan — it's the absence of one. A useful Plan section names something concrete that's different about next time, even if it's small.

**Skipping risk considerations because nothing came up.** An explicit "no risk indicators present this session" in Assessment is worth more on review than silence, which reads as ambiguous rather than reassuring.

A consistent DAP structure, like SOAP, isn't really about satisfying a documentation requirement — it's about making sure a patient's chart, read end to end, actually shows the arc of their treatment rather than a string of similar-sounding entries.
